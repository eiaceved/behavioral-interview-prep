01

How these tests work

A situational judgment test shows you a realistic work scenario and several possible responses. You judge which responses are most and least effective. There's no trick or right personality; firms compare your choices with how their best people would act, based on the firm's values.

Common formats

You'll see one or more of these

  • Rank: order the responses from most to least effective.
  • Most and least: pick the best response and the worst one.
  • Top choices in order: pick the best, then the second best.
  • Rate: score each response on its own, from very effective to very ineffective.

How this page scores you

Close answers earn partial credit

  • Every scenario has an expert ranking of all four responses.
  • Matching a position earns full points. Being one place off earns partial points.
  • Your total becomes a percentage, with a breakdown for eight competencies.
  • Most lost points come from the middle positions, not the best or worst.

Six rules of thumb

1. Fix it and tell the right person

The best responses solve the problem and keep your senior or manager informed.

2. Go through your team

Talk to your senior before the client, and to the person before going over their head.

3. Integrity comes first

Accuracy, confidentiality, and honesty beat speed and pleasing people, every time.

4. Avoid the extremes

Doing nothing and overreacting are usually the two worst options.

5. Own your part

Admit mistakes, flag risks early, and ask for help before a deadline slips.

6. Act like a great first-year

Helpful and proactive, but within your role. Big decisions belong to your manager.

      What firms look for

      The eight competencies this test scores, what strong looks like, and the trap for each.

      Integrity and ethics

      Strong: accurate records, protected client data, mistakes disclosed through your team. Trap: going along with a "small" shortcut because someone senior said so.

      Client focus

      Strong: helpful and responsive, while protecting quality and scope. Trap: saying yes to everything, or no to anything.

      Teamwork and inclusion

      Strong: helps teammates first, gives credit, brings in quieter voices. Trap: going straight over someone's head.

      Ownership and drive

      Strong: owns mistakes, flags risks early with a plan, offers spare time. Trap: hoping a problem fixes itself.

      Communication

      Strong: clarifies vague asks, leads with the answer, tries another channel when stuck. Trap: guessing silently.

      Prioritizing and time

      Strong: makes trade-offs visible and lets the right person decide. Trap: quietly dropping one task or working all night instead of speaking up.

      Adaptability

      Strong: refocuses fast when plans change and takes on stretch work with preparation. Trap: waiting for perfect clarity.

      Learning and feedback

      Strong: thanks people for feedback and acts on it, and asks for it when it's missing. Trap: defending or explaining instead of listening.

      05

      Behavioral round

      The behavioral round is a conversation, not a test. Every question maps to a competency the firm is screening: ownership, teamwork, communication, resilience. Your job is one specific story told in a fixed shape, then stop. Use CARL: one sentence of context, what you did, a result you can point to, and the learning. About ninety seconds, then let them pull the thread.

      The nine questions you are most likely to get

      PwC and Big 4 behavioral staples

      • Tell me about yourself. Sixty seconds, ends on why deals.
      • Why PwC? Why CMAAS? Have one honest reason, not a website quote.
      • Tell me about a time you led a team.
      • A time you worked with someone difficult.
      • A time you failed or missed something. Never a humblebrag.
      • A time you had to learn something fast.
      • A time you handled pressure or an unhappy client.
      • A time you pushed back or faced an integrity question.
      • A time you improved how something worked.

      Rules for every answer

      What separates offers from round files

      • One story per question. Two half-stories score worse than one full one.
      • Say "I", not "we", for the part you actually did.
      • End on the result and the learning, not the drama.
      • Pause before answering. Two seconds of thinking reads as thoughtfulness.
      • Never blame a former manager, teammate, or client.
      • Have numbers: hours saved, people led, deadline hit.

      Your story bank

      FanTAXtic team captain

      Building a two-week plan, running three meetings a week, getting four people to one shared analysis before touching the deck. Covers: leadership, teamwork, deadlines, working across styles.

      Deloitte audit internship

      Real client work, firm tools, and the pace of a professional services floor. Covers: learning fast, attention to detail, what the job actually is.

      Founded Chopped SC

      Taking a cooking club from an idea to a running organization with events and industry guests. Covers: initiative, ownership, building things with no template.

      Twelve years in hospitality

      Guest escalations, packed services, de-escalating angry people while the room keeps moving. Covers: pressure, difficult people, client service, composure.

      First-gen transfer at 28

      Glendale College to USC Leventhal, paying for it yourself, outperforming students ten years younger. Covers: motivation, resilience, why this career is real and not rehearsed.

      Strong answers, in your voice

      These are drafts, not scripts. Read them once, put them away, and say the story your own way. Anything in brackets needs a detail only you know.

      "Tell me about yourself."

      "I spent twelve years in hospitality, most of it running service in busy restaurants, which taught me how to read people and stay calm when everything happens at once. At 28 I went back to school as a first-generation transfer student, and now I am a junior in accounting at USC's Leventhal School. Last summer I interned in audit at Deloitte, I captain my team for Deloitte's FanTAXtic case competition, and I founded a cooking club on campus, Chopped SC. What pulls it together: I like work where analysis and people sit side by side, which is why deal advisory is where I am aiming."

      "Why PwC? Why CMAAS?"

      "The Deloitte internship showed me which part of this work I love: sitting with a real client problem until it is solved. CMAAS is exactly that corner, technical accounting applied to live transactions, explained to people who are not accountants. And I learn best around people who raise the bar, which is what I kept hearing about the Deals practice in Los Angeles." (Swap in your real reason if it is a different one. One specific, honest detail beats three generic ones.)

      "Tell me about a time you led a team."

      "I captain our FanTAXtic team for Deloitte's case competition. At our first practice everyone read the case alone and we were talking past each other. So I wrote a two-week plan: week one, three short meetings where we worked the analysis without AI until everyone reached the same understanding of the objectives; week two, we built the deck together and cleared blockers as they came up. Result: [our deck went in on the Nov 4 deadline and everyone could defend any slide]. What I learned: alignment first, speed second."

      "A time you handled pressure or a difficult person."

      "From restaurants: on a packed Friday, a guest [sent back his meal and started raising his voice], and my manager was buried. I stepped in, let him finish without interrupting, said back to him exactly what he was angry about, fixed what I could fix immediately, and checked back twice before he left. He ended the night happy. What I learned: people want to be heard before they want to be solved, and staying calm is a skill you practice, not a personality trait."

      "A time you failed or missed something."

      "During my internship, [one of my workpapers came back with more review comments than I expected]. I was embarrassed, but I took it to my senior the same day instead of hiding it, fixed every point, and built a short checklist from the comments so the same thing would not happen again. The checklist stuck for the rest of the internship. What I learned: a mistake costs the least when you surface it yourself, early."

      Two questions to ask them

      1. "What does a first-year's first week on the team actually look like?"

      Signals you are already picturing the job, not the title.

      2. "How much client contact do juniors get in CMAAS, and when does it start?"

      Signals your client-facing instinct, which is your differentiator.
